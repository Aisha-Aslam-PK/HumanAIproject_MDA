The UK's scientific community has long stood at the forefront of global research and innovation, bolstered by collaborative networks and funding opportunities provided by EU membership. Brexit has compelled a reevaluation of the UK's role in the international scientific arena, raising concerns about funding, collaboration, and the exchange of knowledge and talent.

The Horizon 2020 program, the EU's key funding instrument for research and innovation, was a vital source of funding for UK scientists, facilitating collaborative projects across member states. With the UK's departure, British researchers faced the prospect of losing access to this crucial funding stream and the collaborative platforms it supports. In response, the UK government committed to underwrite the payment of awards for UK applicants successfully bid for Horizon 2020 funding, ensuring short-term stability.

There is also an emphasis on developing alternative funding sources, including increasing domestic funding for science and research. The government announced ambitious plans to raise research and development (R&D) investment to 2.4% of GDP by 2027. The creation of UK Research and Innovation (UKRI), which brings together the UK's seven research councils, Innovate UK, and Research England, aims to provide a streamlined and strategic approach to funding research in the UK.

Yet, funding is only one element of the broader ecosystem. The freedom of movement under EU membership made the UK a magnet for international talent, which is a cornerstone of successful research. Post-Brexit, the UK has introduced a points-based immigration system, which could restrict the flow of researchers and students to the UK. However, policies such as the Global Talent visa aim to attract leading scientists and researchers to the UK.

Collaboration remains a priority, with the UK seeking to negotiate association to Horizon Europe, the successor to Horizon 2020, which would allow continued participation in EU research funding programs. The UK is also looking to build research relationships beyond Europe, aiming to become a scientific superpower through partnerships with countries like the United States, Japan, and emerging research hubs.

Another area of focus is the regulatory environment. Brexit provides an opportunity for the UK to diverge from EU regulations that are seen as restrictive. This could make the UK a more attractive destination for innovative research, especially in fast-evolving fields such as artificial intelligence and genetics, where agility in regulation can be beneficial.

The long-term success of UK science and research post-Brexit will be determined by the ability to balance the development of independent funding and regulatory frameworks with maintaining and creating strong international partnerships. The UK must continue to attract global talent and ensure that its scientific community remains engaged and integrated within the wider global research landscape.
