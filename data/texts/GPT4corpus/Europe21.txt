The UK fishing industry was at the heart of the Brexit debate, symbolizing sovereignty and the promise of 'taking back control' of British waters. The reality post-Brexit, however, is a complex tapestry of expectation and actuality where gains in some areas are countered by challenges in others.

The departure from the EU's Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) allowed the UK to reassert control over its Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ), extending up to 200 nautical miles from the coast. This change was touted as a boon for British fishers, who anticipated greater quotas and less competition from EU vessels. Indeed, the UK did secure an increase in quota share for several fish stocks in the Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA), but the increment was more modest than many had hoped.

One of the contentious outcomes of the Brexit negotiations was the transitional period, allowing EU fishing vessels continued access to UK waters for a limited time. While this was seen as a necessary compromise, it left some in the UK fishing community feeling that the promises made to them had been partially unfulfilled.

Additionally, new trade barriers have impacted the industry significantly. The UK exports a large proportion of its catch to the EU, and in the post-Brexit scenario, exporters have faced additional red tape, health and safety checks, and customs declarations. These bureaucratic hurdles have led to delays, causing problems for a sector that depends on freshness for premium value, particularly in the case of shellfish.

Access to labor has also been a pressing issue. The fishing fleet often relied on non-UK workers, and stricter immigration rules have made it difficult for some operators to crew their vessels. This has been especially challenging for the processing sector, which historically had a high proportion of EU nationals in its workforce.

The UK government has pledged support for the sector, including funding to modernize fleets and infrastructure, and investment in domestic seafood processing. But adapting to the new landscape requires time and significant adjustment. Fishers are not just navigating regulatory changes but also facing the broader challenges of climate change, which affects fish stocks and migration patterns, and increasing international competition.

The future of the UK fishing industry post-Brexit is still being charted. While there is potential for growth and a more sustainable approach to fisheries management, the sector finds itself in a transitional phase, negotiating the currents of change that Brexit has undoubtedly set in motion.
