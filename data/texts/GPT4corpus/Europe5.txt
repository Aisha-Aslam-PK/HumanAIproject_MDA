The tension between national sovereignty and supranational governance has been at the heart of Britain's complex relationship with the European Union. This tension reflects a broader philosophical debate about the nature of state power and governance in an era of increasing global interconnectivity.

The allure of a single Europe has always been tempered for Britain by a staunch defense of its sovereignty. This is evident in the recurring debates within the British polity about the extent to which EU laws and regulations should supersede national legislation. The concept of supranationalism, central to the EU’s mission, posits that member states benefit from surrendering some degree of sovereignty to a higher authority in exchange for the advantages of membership, including access to a single market and collective security arrangements.

However, for many in Britain, supranationalism is seen as an infringement upon self-governance. The principle of parliamentary sovereignty — that the UK Parliament is the supreme legal authority capable of creating or ending any law — often seemed at odds with EU membership, where European institutions have the power to make laws binding on all member states. This was particularly challenging for the British legal system, which is based on common law traditions that emphasize the rulings of domestic courts.

Eurosceptics argued that EU membership entailed a democratic deficit, with decisions affecting the UK being made by distant bureaucrats rather than by elected British officials. They pointed to the European Commission's right of initiative as a fundamental challenge to British democratic processes, as it meant that only the Commission could propose legislation in many areas.

Proponents of the EU within Britain, meanwhile, countered that the nation's sovereignty was actually enhanced by EU membership. They highlighted how the EU’s decision-making structures include the Council of the European Union, which consists of government ministers from each member state, and the European Parliament, which is directly elected by EU citizens. According to this view, being part of these institutions allowed Britain to shape policies and legislation at the European level, thus extending its own sovereignty through influence and cooperation.

Despite these arguments, the concern that EU membership meant a continual erosion of national sovereignty was a significant factor in the Brexit vote. This reflects a broader trend of resurgent nationalism across the globe, where the values of independence and national identity are reasserting themselves against the backdrop of supranational governance structures.

In conclusion, Britain’s struggle with the concept of sovereignty versus supranationalism in the context of a united Europe highlights a fundamental dilemma faced by nation-states today: how to balance the benefits of collective action and integration with the desire for national autonomy and control. The resolution of this dilemma is still unfolding as Britain forges a new role for itself outside the EU, seeking to maintain the advantages of cooperation with European neighbors while regaining the full measure of parliamentary sovereignty long held as a cornerstone of its national identity.
