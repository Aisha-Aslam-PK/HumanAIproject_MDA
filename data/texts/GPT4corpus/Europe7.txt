Britain’s legal system is a tapestry of centuries-old statutes and common law, embodying the country's profound respect for the sovereignty of its legal institutions. Joining the European Community introduced a dualist system where European law held primacy over national laws in certain areas, a revolutionary shift for a country that holds the unwritten constitution and parliamentary sovereignty in high regard.

The incorporation of European law into the British legal system was a contentious aspect of EU membership. The European Court of Justice (ECJ) became the supreme legal body in matters of EU law, and its decisions were binding on all member states, including the UK. This arrangement was seen by many as a direct challenge to the supremacy of the British courts and the principle that no parliament could pass laws that future parliaments could not change.

EU legislation concerning human rights also played a pivotal role in this complex relationship. The European Convention on Human Rights, although not an EU document but one of the Council of Europe, had a significant impact on British law through the Human Rights Act 1998. This act required British courts to give effect to the rights enshrined in the Convention, further diluting the concept of legal sovereignty as interpreted by Eurosceptics.

Throughout its membership, Britain often found itself at odds with the EU's legal framework. Notable disputes included the Working Time Directive and the Charter of Fundamental Rights. Such directives and regulations were perceived as intrusions into domestic legal autonomy, dictating policies that some argued should be left to national governments.

Brexit was partly a legal revolt, aiming to restore the ultimate authority of British law and the courts. The repatriation of legal powers was a key argument for the leave campaign, promising an end to the jurisdiction of the ECJ over UK affairs. The Great Repeal Bill, formally known as the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018, was symbolic of this desire to re-establish legal sovereignty, as it aimed to convert EU law into British law and then amend or repeal it as deemed necessary by the UK Parliament.

However, disentangling UK law from EU law proved to be a Herculean task. Decades of intertwined legislation could not be easily separated, and the influence of European law will likely continue to be felt for years to come. Moreover, any future trade deals with the EU will require some level of alignment with EU regulations, suggesting that complete legal sovereignty remains somewhat idealistic.

In conclusion, the quest for legal autonomy has been at the heart of Britain’s sovereignty concerns regarding a single Europe. While the notion of reclaiming full legal control was a powerful driver behind Brexit, the practical realities of legal interdependence have highlighted the complex balance between sovereignty and the benefits of collective legislation. Britain’s ongoing challenge will be to redefine its legal relationship with the EU while preserving the domestic legal traditions that are a cornerstone of its national identity.
