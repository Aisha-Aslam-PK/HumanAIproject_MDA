The UK's scientific community has long been a leader in global research and innovation, supported by collaborations and funding from EU membership. Brexit has forced a reassessment of the UK's position in the international scientific field, raising concerns about funding, cooperation, and the sharing of knowledge and talent.

The Horizon 2020 program, the main funding tool for research and innovation in the EU, was a key source of funding for UK scientists. It enabled collaborative projects among member states. After the UK left the EU, British researchers faced the possibility of losing access to this important funding and the collaborative opportunities it provided. In response, the UK government promised to cover payment of awards for UK applicants who successfully bid for Horizon 2020 funding, ensuring some short-term stability.

There is also a focus on finding alternative funding sources, including increasing domestic funding for science and research. The government announced plans to raise research and development (R&D) investment to 2.4% of GDP by 2027. The creation of UK Research and Innovation (UKRI), which combines the UK's seven research councils, Innovate UK, and Research England, aims to streamline funding for research in the UK.

However, funding is just one part of the larger ecosystem. The free movement allowed by EU membership made the UK an attractive place for international talent, which is essential for successful research. After Brexit, the UK implemented a points-based immigration system that might limit the flow of researchers and students. Still, initiatives like the Global Talent visa are designed to attract top scientists and researchers to the UK.

Collaboration continues to be a priority, with the UK aiming to negotiate its association with Horizon Europe, the successor to Horizon 2020. This would enable continued participation in EU research funding programs. The UK is also looking to establish research partnerships beyond Europe, hoping to become a scientific leader by collaborating with countries like the United States, Japan, and emerging research centers.

Another focus area is the regulatory environment. Brexit offers a chance for the UK to move away from EU regulations viewed as restrictive. This could make the UK a more appealing location for innovative research, especially in rapidly changing fields like artificial intelligence and genetics, where flexible regulations can be beneficial.

The long-term success of UK science and research after Brexit will depend on balancing the creation of independent funding and regulatory systems with maintaining strong international partnerships. The UK must continue to attract global talent and keep its scientific community engaged in the broader global research landscape.
