The UK's exit from the European Union marked a major change in both political and legal terms. With Brexit, the UK removed itself from the authority of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) and the direct impact of EU law. This separation signifies a major shift in the legal framework governing the UK’s operations, both at home and in its foreign relationships.

For many years, EU law held priority over national law in member states, ensuring laws were consistently applied throughout the Union. The Great Repeal Bill, officially called the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018, was key to this transition. It ended the priority of EU law in the UK and incorporated existing EU legislation into UK law. This was a complicated task due to the vast amount of law built up during membership.

One immediate effect has been the need to revise laws originally created within the EU framework. Areas like environmental law, workers' rights, and consumer protection, which were deeply tied to EU directives and regulations, now require the UK to create its own standards or choose to keep alignment with EU laws for consistency and ease of trade.

The UK’s courts, which had grown used to referring matters to the CJEU for final rulings on EU law, must now interpret these laws without relying on the CJEU. This raises concerns about how EU case law will influence UK rulings and how far UK courts might stray from established European interpretations.

Brexit also involves redefining the UK's legal relationship with the EU regarding trade, security, and many other areas where cooperation remains vital. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement, reached at the end of 2020, established the basis for this new legal relationship, but many specifics still need to be clarified in various sectors.

On a global scale, the UK can now create its own trade deals and legal frameworks. This regained sovereignty comes with the responsibility to manage it wisely. The new freedom allows the UK to customize its legal standards to better meet its economic and social needs, possibly diverging from EU norms when it makes sense.

For UK lawyers, Brexit has led to shifts in practice. Those who focused on EU law have had to adjust, and the status of UK lawyers working in the EU, as well as EU lawyers in the UK, has changed. The mutual recognition of professional qualifications and cross-border practice has become more complicated and uncertain.

The full effects of these legal changes will take time to fully emerge as the UK forges its independent path. While supporters of Brexit celebrate this newfound autonomy, it also brings a responsibility to ensure that the legal systems and frameworks established continue to protect citizens' rights, foster economic growth, and support effective international cooperation.
