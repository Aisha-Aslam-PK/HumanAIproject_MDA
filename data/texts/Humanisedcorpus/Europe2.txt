The debate about Britain's sovereignty in a united Europe revolves around balancing national self-rule with international partnerships. The EU is a unique project in regional cooperation, where national interests often mix with European policies. For Britain, being part of this project has sparked serious discussions about its sovereignty’s nature and future.

The principle of subsidiarity in the EU states that decisions should be made as close to the citizen as possible, aiming to address sovereignty worries. However, many in Britain felt that Brussels was taking over issues better managed by local or national governments, leading to growing skepticism. Critics say that despite subsidiarity, the EU’s reach has grown into areas like environmental rules, financial services, and immigration, which many Britons believe should be managed solely by the nation.

Britain’s Eurosceptics often point to the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) and the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) as clear examples of lost sovereignty. They argue that these policies dictate terms to British farmers and fishermen while causing economic waste and environmental harm. Frustration with these policies became a rallying cry for the Brexit campaign, emphasizing the wish for Britain to regain control over its agricultural and fishing resources.

Conversely, some argue that in today’s world, sovereignty improves through cooperation. They believe challenges like climate change and terrorism ignore national borders and need cooperative solutions. While in the EU, Britain could influence European laws and policies, shaping them to suit its interests. This could be seen as a form of "shared sovereignty."

The idea of "pooled sovereignty" suggests that working with other nations can increase a country's influence beyond what it could achieve alone. The EU's collective bargaining power in trade talks illustrates this, providing member states with access to markets and conditions that would be hard to obtain on their own. For Britain, this aspect of EU membership was a strategic benefit, particularly with the rise of global powers like China and India.

On the other hand, critics of pooled sovereignty highlight the EU's perceived democratic deficit. The fact that unelected officials in Brussels can make rules that affect British citizens challenges democratic values for many in the UK. The push for direct accountability and democratic legitimacy was a major reason behind the desire to leave the EU.

In summary, Britain’s tension with the idea of sovereignty in a united Europe mirrors larger conflicts between nationalism and globalization. The key issue centers on how to effectively manage autonomy alongside partnership, independence, and interdependence. As Britain continues to redefine its relationship with the EU after Brexit, it serves as an example of the challenges and appeals of national sovereignty in a more connected world.
