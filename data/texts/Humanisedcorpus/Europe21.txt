The UK fishing industry was central to the Brexit debate, representing sovereignty and the idea of "taking back control" of British waters. However, the reality after Brexit is a complicated mix of expectations and outcomes. Gains in some areas are met with challenges in others.

Leaving the EU's Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) allowed the UK to regain control over its Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ), which extends up to 200 nautical miles from the coast. This change was seen as a benefit for British fishers, who looked forward to bigger quotas and less competition from EU vessels. Indeed, the UK did get an increase in quota share for some fish stocks in the Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA), but the increase was smaller than many had hoped.

One of the debated results of the Brexit negotiations was the transitional period, which allowed EU fishing vessels to continue accessing UK waters for a limited time. While this compromise was deemed necessary, some in the UK fishing community felt the promises made to them were not fully met.

New trade barriers have also affected the industry significantly. The UK exports a large part of its catch to the EU, and post-Brexit, exporters face extra paperwork, health and safety checks, and customs declarations. These bureaucratic issues have caused delays, which are problematic for a sector relying on freshness for high value, especially in the case of shellfish.

Access to labor has become a major concern. The fishing fleet often depended on non-UK workers, and stricter immigration rules have made it hard for some operators to staff their vessels. This issue has been particularly difficult for the processing sector, which traditionally employed many EU nationals.

The UK government has promised support for the industry, including funding to modernize fleets and infrastructure, as well as investment in domestic seafood processing. But adjusting to the new environment takes time and significant changes. Fishers are not just dealing with regulatory updates; they are also facing bigger challenges from climate change, which affects fish stocks and migration patterns, along with rising international competition.

The future of the UK fishing industry after Brexit is still being defined. While there is potential for growth and a more sustainable approach to fisheries management, the sector is currently in a transitional phase, navigating the changes that Brexit has undeniably brought about.
