Brexit has brought a significant change, affecting trade, regulations, security, and law enforcement. Before Brexit, the United Kingdom had full access to various EU security tools, including the European Arrest Warrant (EAW), Europol, and the Schengen Information System (SIS II). These tools are essential for fighting cross-border crime and terrorism. With the end of EU membership, the UK now needed to create a new security relationship, balancing its national sovereignty with the need for cooperation.

The EU's security systems allowed for quick and smooth collaboration between law enforcement agencies of member states. The EAW enabled rapid extradition of suspects, while Europol facilitated intelligence sharing and coordinated operations against organized crime and terrorism. SIS II offered real-time alerts about wanted individuals and missing persons. However, after Brexit, the UK lost direct access to these tools, raising concerns about the effectiveness and speed of future cooperation.

Instead, the UK and the EU have worked out alternative arrangements under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA). While these new arrangements do not fully match the capabilities the UK once had, they establish a framework for ongoing cooperation. The UK still belongs to Europol and Eurojust, but it has less influence and lacks direct access to databases. It can no longer issue or receive EAWs and must rely on a different surrender agreement that involves longer timelines and increased legal complexities.

This new security setup has created delays and complications in data sharing and criminal extradition. UK law enforcement agencies now face longer procedures to access crucial information and must deal with additional bureaucratic challenges when cooperating on international crime.

The UK government has highlighted the regained sovereignty over laws and borders as a boost to security. They argue that the UK can now customize its security and immigration policies without needing to follow EU rules. However, the success of these independent policies relies on the ability to establish new bilateral agreements and maintain intelligence-sharing relationships with both EU and non-EU countries.

The balance between sovereignty and security cooperation raises important questions about the UK's strategy against international crime and terrorism in the future. Maintaining strong ties with the EU is vital, as is building a global network of security partnerships.

The challenges ahead are considerable as the UK navigates this complex landscape. Finding ways to create effective security measures while operating outside the EU's legal framework will be essential for the UK's internal security and its role in international law enforcement.
