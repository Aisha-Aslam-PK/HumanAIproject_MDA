Britain's view on a united Europe is rooted in its unique history. This background has influenced how the British see sovereignty and their role in European integration. The country’s history as a global empire and its identity as an island nation have fostered a strong sense of independence, often conflicting with the idea of a united Europe. 

From the beginning, Britain's entry into the European Economic Community (EEC) in 1973 was marked by uncertainty. Unlike founding members whose economies needed stability after the war, Britain joined with a focus on economic benefits rather than political unity. This created a back-and-forth relationship with Europe, where Britain welcomed economic advantages but remained cautious about deeper political ties.

This skepticism stems from Britain's history as a global power. Having once enjoyed significant influence worldwide, the idea of sharing sovereignty within Europe felt contrary to the vision of a sovereign Britain. The impact of World War II added to this perspective; Britain’s role in defeating tyranny fostered national pride in its democratic system and legal framework, which many believed should remain intact.

The idea of a "Common Market" was generally acceptable to the British public, but as the EEC transitioned into the European Union with greater political goals, discomfort grew. The Maastricht Treaty of 1992, which aimed for more political and economic integration, sparked significant debate. The introduction of the euro and the move towards a shared foreign and security policy raised questions in Britain about national sovereignty and identity.

During its time in the EU, Britain negotiated several opt-outs from major EU policies, such as the euro and the Schengen Area. These decisions highlighted Britain’s cautious approach to European integration. They represented both an insistence on sovereignty and a practical reflection of its unease with EU policy developments. 

Brexit, the result of Britain’s mixed feelings about European integration, is seen by those who voted to leave as a chance to reclaim lost sovereignty and to restore a vision of Britain that emphasizes self-reliance and a global perspective. However, the challenges after Brexit have shown that the idea of complete sovereignty is hard to align with the complexities of today’s international relationships.

In summary, Britain’s understanding of sovereignty and its attitude towards European unity must consider the historical factors that have shaped its identity. The legacy of empire, experiences of wartime independence, and a geographical sense of separation from the continent have all played a role in forming a distinct British view on sovereignty, one that has both clashed with and aligned itself to the ideals of a united Europe.
