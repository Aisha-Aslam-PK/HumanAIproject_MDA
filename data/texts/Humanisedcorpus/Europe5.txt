The tension between national sovereignty and supranational governance has been central to Britain's complicated relationship with the European Union. This tension reflects a wider debate about state power and governance in a time of growing global interconnectivity.

Britain has always valued its sovereignty, which has complicated the allure of a unified Europe. This is clear in the ongoing discussions in British politics about how much EU laws and regulations should override national laws. Supranationalism, which is key to the EU’s mission, suggests that countries gain from giving up some sovereignty to a higher authority in return for benefits, such as access to a single market and shared security.

However, many in Britain view supranationalism as a threat to self-governance. The idea of parliamentary sovereignty—where the UK Parliament is the highest legal authority that can create or end any law—often conflicted with EU membership. In the EU, European institutions can create laws that all member states must follow. This posed challenges for the British legal system, which relies on common law traditions and domestic court rulings.

Eurosceptics argued that EU membership created a democratic deficit. They believed that decisions impacting the UK were made by distant bureaucrats instead of elected British officials. They pointed to the European Commission's right to propose legislation in many areas as a serious challenge to British democratic processes.

Supporters of the EU in Britain countered that EU membership actually strengthened national sovereignty. They pointed out that the EU’s decision-making bodies, like the Council of the European Union, which consists of government ministers from each member state, and the European Parliament, directly elected by EU citizens, allowed Britain to influence policies and laws at the European level. From this perspective, being part of these institutions enhanced Britain’s sovereignty through influence and cooperation.

Still, concerns that EU membership gradually weakened national sovereignty played a big role in the Brexit vote. This reflects a wider trend of increasing nationalism around the world, where values like independence and national identity are pushing back against supranational governance.

In conclusion, Britain’s struggle with sovereignty versus supranationalism in a united Europe highlights a major dilemma for nation-states today: how to balance the advantages of collective action and integration with the need for national autonomy and control. The outcome of this dilemma is still developing as Britain shapes its new role outside the EU, aiming to keep the benefits of cooperation with its European neighbors while reclaiming the full extent of parliamentary sovereignty, which has long been a key part of its national identity.
