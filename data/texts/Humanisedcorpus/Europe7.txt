Britain's legal system consists of centuries-old laws and common law, showing the country's deep respect for its legal institutions. Joining the European Community introduced a dual legal system where European law took precedence over national laws in certain areas. This was a significant change for a country that values its unwritten constitution and parliamentary sovereignty.

The incorporation of European law into the British legal system was a controversial part of EU membership. The European Court of Justice (ECJ) became the highest legal authority in EU matters, and its decisions were binding for all member states, including the UK. Many saw this as a challenge to the authority of British courts and the principle that no parliament can pass laws that future parliaments cannot change.

EU laws about human rights also played an important role in this complex relationship. The European Convention on Human Rights, while not an EU document but part of the Council of Europe, significantly affected British law through the Human Rights Act 1998. This act required British courts to enforce the rights included in the Convention, further weakening the notion of legal sovereignty as interpreted by Eurosceptics.

During its EU membership, Britain often clashed with the EU's legal system. Notable disputes included the Working Time Directive and the Charter of Fundamental Rights. Many viewed these directives and regulations as intruding on domestic legal independence, imposing policies that some believed should be decided by national governments.

Brexit represented, in part, a legal push to restore the ultimate authority of British law and its courts. The return of legal powers was a key argument for the leave campaign, promising to end the ECJ's control over UK matters. The Great Repeal Bill, officially known as the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018, symbolized this desire to re-establish legal sovereignty, as it aimed to convert EU law into British law and allow the UK Parliament to amend or repeal it as needed.

However, separating UK law from EU law turned out to be an enormous challenge. Decades of intertwined legislation could not be easily untangled, and the effects of European law will likely continue for years. Moreover, any future trade agreements with the EU will require some alignment with EU regulations, indicating that complete legal sovereignty may be unrealistic.

In summary, the pursuit of legal independence has been central to Britain's concerns about sovereignty in a united Europe. While the idea of regaining full legal control was a strong motivation for Brexit, the realities of legal interdependence have shown the complex balance between sovereignty and the advantages of collective legislation. Britain's ongoing challenge will be to redefine its legal relationship with the EU while maintaining the domestic legal traditions that are essential to its national identity.
