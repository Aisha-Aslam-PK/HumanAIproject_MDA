The use of gene editing in in vitro fertilization (IVF) procedures pushes the boundaries of reproductive choice into new and complex areas. The ethical issues surrounding these choices affect not just families but society as a whole, as they relate to the very core of human life and the natural order.

When looking at choice in the context of gene editing and IVF, various ethical views come into play. From a practical standpoint, the largest benefit for the most people suggests that eliminating genetic diseases is a clear moral need. However, this idea gets more complicated when we consider the potential for non-medical genetic enhancements. Should parents have the option to choose traits that are seen as 'desirable,' like increased intelligence or better physical abilities? This shift from treating illness to improving traits raises serious ethical questions about parenthood, children's rights, and the kind of society we want to build.

The principle of autonomy is central to modern bioethics. It supports the right of individuals to make informed choices about their health care. In gene-edited IVF, this means that prospective parents should be allowed to decide what genetic editing their future child might experience. However, parental autonomy must be weighed against the future autonomy of the child, who cannot agree to these decisions. This conflict lies at the center of the ethical challenges related to gene-edited IVF.

Kantian ethics, which stress duty and the inherent value of individuals, warns against any genetic manipulation that turns human life into an object or commodity. From this viewpoint, designing a child with specific traits may be morally troubling, as it fails to recognize the child as an individual with their own worth and instead treats them as a product of parental wishes.

Additionally, there is a broader social ethical aspect to think about. If gene editing becomes widely used, society will face the risk of worsening inequalities. The cost of these procedures might create a new kind of social divide, separating people not only by wealth and opportunity but also by their biology.

From a virtue ethics perspective, the character and intentions of those making gene editing decisions in IVF are crucial. Parents, doctors, and policymakers should be guided by the virtues of humility, caution, and fairness. The arrogance of trying to control the genetic makeup of future generations should be balanced with the understanding that human knowledge and complex biological systems have limits and uncertainties.

In summary, the ethical issues surrounding gene-edited IVF are complicated and filled with moral challenges. While the appeal of removing genetic diseases is strong, the larger effects of introducing genetic choice require a careful and principled approach. The choices made today will set important precedents for future generations, so it is vital that these decisions are made within a thoughtful ethical framework that respects individual welfare, children's rights, and the society we want to create.
