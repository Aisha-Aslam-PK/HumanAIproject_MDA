The introduction of gene editing in IVF raises complex international ethical and policy challenges. These issues are intensified by how quickly science is advancing compared to the slower pace of policy making and agreement. It is essential to align ethical views and legal systems across different cultures and countries to responsibly navigate this new field.

The potential of gene editing is worldwide, and so are its ethical dilemmas. Some nations may accept these technologies, while others may reject them based on moral, religious, or philosophical beliefs. This difference can create a divided international landscape where scientific communities face varying regulations.

The risk of 'genetic tourism' demonstrates the need for global discussion and policy-making. If one country bans certain gene editing procedures while another allows them, individuals may travel to access technologies that are not permitted in their home countries. This situation could lead to a divide where the wealthy can seek out their preferred genetic options, while others are limited by their citizenship or financial situation.

Worldwide cooperation is essential to tackle these issues. Organizations like the World Health Organization and the United Nations could help promote discussion and set guidelines. We urgently need an international convention on gene editing to inform national laws and support a collaborative approach to research, development, and clinical use.

A key part of this global governance is ensuring fair access to gene editing technologies. A broad global strategy could help avoid a scenario where only a select few benefit from scientific advancements, leaving the majority vulnerable to genetic conditions that could be eliminated.

Additionally, we must consider how to handle unexpected results from gene editing. An international registry of gene editing procedures and outcomes could be valuable in tracking long-term effects and ensuring openness in research and clinical practice. This would also promote shared knowledge and minimize redundant efforts among different research institutions.

The discussion on harmonizing ethics and policy should include not only scientists and policymakers but also ethicists, religious leaders, patient advocacy groups, and the public. It is important that this conversation reflects the wide range of perspectives and values in our global community.

In closing, integrating gene editing into IVF practices demands a high level of international cooperation. We must balance embracing the benefits of scientific progress with protecting against ethical problems. The future relies on our collective ability to create policies that are adaptable to scientific growth while strong enough to address ethical and social issues. Bringing these efforts together worldwide will help ensure that the benefits of gene editing are equitably shared, paving the way for a future where genetic diseases are eliminated and preserving the rich diversity and values that define our humanity.
