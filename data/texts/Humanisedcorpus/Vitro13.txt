The use of gene-editing technologies like CRISPR in IVF practices could change human reproduction and genetics significantly. While the medical community agrees on the potential to wipe out hereditary diseases, the effects of this technology reach far beyond healthcare.

If gene editing in IVF becomes common, we may see a drop in genetic disorders. Families with a history of these diseases might find hope, as the worry about passing on serious conditions decreases. This could lessen the overall healthcare burden, as fewer people might need long-term treatment for genetic conditions that were once untreatable.

On the other hand, using gene editing in IVF brings up important questions about our values and norms. It may change how we conceive children, moving from natural methods full of unpredictability to a more controlled process. Parents might feel pressured to choose genetic changes to prevent diseases or provide their children with certain advantages.

The availability of gene editing could widen social gaps. If these procedures are costly, only wealthy individuals might access them, creating a divide where rich families can 'improve' their children. This could strengthen existing social and economic inequalities.

The idea of genetically modified people also makes us rethink identity and human diversity. Our understanding of "normal" might change, as traits once seen as natural variations could be viewed as fixable issues. This shift could affect how society values individual differences and might lead to less acceptance of those who are not genetically modified, whether due to personal choice or lack of means.

Additionally, gene editing could influence human evolution. If we regularly edit out specific genes from the human genome, we risk losing genetic diversity that is essential for our species' survival and adaptability. The full effects of these changes are unclear and might not be realized for generations.

On a global scale, the rules governing gene editing in IVF will likely differ widely. This could result in 'genetic tourism,' where people travel to countries with looser regulations for gene-editing procedures. Such a scenario could create uneven ethical standards and complicate the global management of biotechnology.

In summary, the societal impacts of adding gene editing to IVF are extensive and intricate. While there are promising benefits, like fewer genetic diseases, there are also serious risks, including greater social inequality and challenges to our understanding of diversity and identity. To navigate this new landscape, we need ethical discussions, public involvement, and international collaboration to ensure these technologies are used fairly and responsibly. As we enter this new era of genetic possibilities, we should approach it with both hope and caution, keeping our responsibility to future generations in mind.
