The development of gene editing in IVF raises biological, ethical, and important socioeconomic questions. As this technology advances, it might become more accessible to those who can pay for it. This could create a society that is divided not only by wealth and social status but also by genetic enhancements.

The possibility of gene-edited IVF treatments worsens existing inequalities in healthcare access. Fertility treatments are already expensive and usually not covered by insurance, which makes them hard to reach for lower-income individuals and families. If gene editing becomes part of standard IVF procedures, it may bring even higher costs, increasing the gap between those who can afford new medical options and those who cannot.

This financial barrier may have lasting social effects. If gene editing can improve certain traits, a situation might develop where wealthy individuals can give their children advantages, like resistance to certain diseases or better cognitive abilities. This could lead to a society divided by genetics, making class divides worse and introducing new forms of discrimination based on genetic traits.

Additionally, the global spread of these technologies will likely be unequal. Wealthier countries and individuals will gain access first. This difference could reinforce global inequalities, with developed nations advancing in biotechnology while poorer nations stay behind. The outcome might be a kind of 'genetic imperialism,' where genetics influence geopolitical power.

To tackle these socioeconomic disparities, we need active public policies and ethical oversight. This might include regulating the prices of treatments to make them more accessible or setting up public funds to help those who cannot afford these medical procedures. Education is crucial in raising awareness about gene editing's implications. It helps people make informed choices and push for fair policies.

Public policy must also address the international differences in access to gene editing technologies. Agreements might be needed to share information and resources. This way, advancements in biotechnology can benefit everyone, not just a select few.

In summary, the rise of gene editing in IVF could highlight socioeconomic inequality. Without careful thought and active steps, we risk deepening social divides and creating new kinds of inequality. The future of gene editing in IVF shouldn’t be left to market forces alone; it should prioritize social justice and fair access to medical advancements. Policymakers, ethicists, and the global community need to collaborate to ensure these technologies bring people together, creating a more fair future for all.
